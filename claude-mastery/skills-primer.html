Claude Mastery · Module 01

Installable Expertise

A skill is a folder Claude loads when your task calls for it. You write the expertise once; Claude applies it every time after.

The object

It is a folder. That's the whole format.

No SDK, no build step, no registration. A skill is a directory with one required file and whatever supporting material the job needs.

my-skill/
├── SKILL.md          # required: instructions + frontmatter
├── references/       # deep material, read only when needed
│   └── style-guide.md
├── scripts/          # code Claude runs instead of improvising
│   └── validate.py
└── templates/        # files Claude fills in
    └── report.md

SKILL.md opens with YAML frontmatter, then plain Markdown instructions:

---
name: quarterly-report
description: Generate the quarterly business review in house
  format. Use when the user asks for a QBR, quarterly report, board
  update, or business review. Pulls figures from metrics.csv and
  never invents numbers.
---

# Quarterly Report

1. Read metrics.csv, compute quarter-over-quarter deltas.
2. Run scripts/validate.py to confirm the numbers reconcile.
3. Fill templates/report.md, following references/style-guide.md.

The mechanism

Progressive disclosure is the entire point

This is the part most users never learn, and it's what makes skills different from every other way of customizing Claude. A skill loads in layers, and only the layer your task needs ever reaches the context window.

Compare that to the alternatives. Everything you put in project instructions or a CLAUDE.md file is paid for on every single message, relevant or not. Skills are the only customization mechanism that gives Claude effectively unbounded expertise at near-zero standing cost.

That property is why the rest of this page matters. It means you can keep building, forever, without ever paying for the skills you aren't using right now.

Reach

Write once, works everywhere

The same folder format runs on every Claude surface. The skill you build in Claude Code works when you upload it to claude.ai, and when your company later automates the workflow through the API.

Two ways a skill fires. Automatically, when Claude reads your request and matches it against every installed skill's description. Or explicitly, as a slash command in Claude Code (/quarterly-report). Explicit is a guarantee; automatic is the convenience you're aiming for.

Judgment

When it's a skill, and when it isn't

The reliable signal: you have typed the same instructions twice. The third time is you doing a skill's job by hand.

Four more signals. There is a right way to do it, with steps and checks. The output must match a format or voice. Claude needs knowledge it can't have — your internal API, your client's quirks, your family's constraints. Or part of the job is deterministic, and a script should do it.

Keep the two axes straight and you'll out-architect most users: CLAUDE.md is always loaded, so keep it tiny. Skills load on demand, so they can be huge.

Applied

Two you could build today

Work

The Monday status update

You produce some recurring artifact — a status update, a client summary, a standup report. Every week you re-explain the format, the tone, what your manager cares about. That re-explanation is a skill, still running in interpreted mode. Compile it:

description: Write my Monday status update for my manager. Use
  when I ask for my weekly update, status report, or Monday summary.

## Rules
- Three sections: Shipped / In progress / Blocked+Risks.
- Lead every bullet with the outcome, not the activity.
  ("Cut page load 40%" not "Worked on performance.")
- Blocked items name the person or decision needed.
- Under 200 words. My manager reads on a phone.
- No "just", no "hopefully".

Monday becomes: paste raw notes, type weekly update, get a finished draft. Ten minutes to write, saved every week forever, and more consistent than when you prompted from scratch.

Personal

The trip planner

Personal life is where skills are most underused. Anything you do repeatedly with rules attached qualifies.

description: Plan family trips my way. Use when I ask to plan
  a trip, vacation, itinerary, or getaway.

## Constraints (non-negotiable)
- Two adults, one 6-year-old: one big activity per day max,
  hotel pool required, first dinner near the hotel.
- Hotels under $250/night, breakfast included preferred.
- I hate: hop-on-hop-off buses, QR-code-only menus.
- Always output: day-by-day table, booking checklist with
  deadlines, packing list keyed to the forecast.

Now “plan us four days in Osaka in November” lands tailored on the first try, from your phone, carrying twenty preferences you would never bother typing. The same shape works for meal planning around your diet, a monthly budget review that reads your bank export, gift research, or a homework helper with your rule about not giving the answer away.

The difference

Six things the top 1% do

01

The description is the product

Most skills fail at retrieval, not content: Claude never loads them because the description doesn't match how you actually phrase things. At session start, that description is the only thing Claude sees. Amateurs write labels. Experts write trigger specifications.

# Never fires
description: A skill for better writing.

# Fires exactly when it should
description: Remove AI writing patterns from prose. Use when
  drafting, editing, or polishing any text — emails, docs,
  posts — or when the user says it "sounds like AI" or
  "too wordy". Do not use for code comments.

The formula: what it does, when to use it, the literal words you'd say, and when not to. Then test it — phrase the task three ways in fresh sessions and check it loads each time. If it doesn't, tune the description, not the body.

02

Ship scripts, not prose

Experts push every deterministic step into a bundled script and reserve the model for judgment. Anthropic's own document skills work this way: the Excel skill doesn't describe how to build a spreadsheet, it ships Python that does it flawlessly.

Ask of every step: does this have exactly one correct output? Parsing a CSV, validating totals, renaming files — script. Deciding what the numbers mean — model. Scripts are faster, cost almost no context, and never hallucinate.

03

The compile loop

This is the big one. Top users treat prompts as source and skills as the compiled binary. The habit: the moment a session produces a great result, end it with “turn what we just did into a skill.” Claude Code ships a skill-creator that interviews you, drafts the folder, writes the trigger description, and evaluates it.

Every correction you make once becomes a rule Claude never breaks again. Average users have the same conversation every week and lose the learning each time — the session ends and the coaching evaporates. Top users convert coaching into infrastructure, and the gap compounds weekly.

04

Test them like software

Version-controlled, changelogged, evaluated. Run realistic tasks with and without the skill, compare outputs, measure whether the description triggers reliably. When a skill misbehaves, don't rewrite it — add the failing case the way you'd add a regression test.

05

Small skills compose

One giant do-everything skill performs worse than five sharp ones, because Claude loads exactly what the task needs and combines them — brand-voice plus the PowerPoint skill plus a data-analysis skill, in a single request. Build a library of single-purpose tools, not a Swiss Army knife with fifty blades open at once.

06

Skills are how teams scale taste

A skill committed to .claude/skills/ ships to every teammate on git pull; admins can push skills across an organization. Your best reviewer's checklist and your best writer's voice, encoded once and applied by everyone. That's the difference between a style guide nobody reads and one that enforces itself.

Without skills, your output quality depends on how well you prompt today — and your best prompting evaporates when the chat ends.

With a library, prompting shrinks to intent: “do the weekly report” replaces three paragraphs, because the three paragraphs are installed. Quality becomes a floor instead of a coin flip, since the skill applies rule seven every time, including the one you'd forget. Delegation becomes trustworthy, so you review outcomes rather than supervising mechanics.

And it compounds. Week-ten you is served by nine weeks of accumulated, tested expertise. That's the real moat: the top 1% aren't better prompters, they've stopped re-prompting.

This week

Six moves, in order

  1. Read a real skill end to end — SKILL.md first, then its references/. Note the router-plus-references shape.
  2. List the last three instructions you've given Claude more than twice. Those are your first three skills.
  3. Build one with /skill-creator. Keep SKILL.md under 100 lines.
  4. Test the trigger: fresh session, phrase the task three ways, tune until it fires three times out of three.
  5. Find one deterministic step in it and move that step into scripts/.
  6. For one week, end every good session with: “Should any of this become a skill? If yes, draft it.”

That last habit is the one that does the work. Everything else on this page is technique; that one is compounding.
